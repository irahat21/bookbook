Paper Boats and Thunder
Original poems written for the BookOrbit AI prototype.

CHAPTER 1: Rain

Paper Boats

My brother folds the morning news
into a boat with a pointed bow.
The headlines sail along the gutter:
prices rising, rain tomorrow, how
a minister has lost his way.
We chase them to the river's mouth
and watch the bad news float away,
the small white fleet heading south.

Thunder

The thunder is my grandfather
moving furniture upstairs,
dragging the old oak wardrobe
across the bedroom floor,
dropping his boots one after the other,
coughing, then going quiet.
We count between the light and sound:
one mile, two miles, gone to bed.

Umbrella

All afternoon the umbrella waited
by the door like a folded bird.
It has one job and one season.
When the rain arrives, it opens its wings
and is happy in the only way it knows:
being needed, being wet,
holding a small dry room above my head.

Puddle

The puddle in the schoolyard
keeps a copy of the sky.
Clouds pass through it carefully,
a bird goes upside down,
and when I jump the whole world shakes
and breaks into a thousand pieces,
then slowly, patiently, mends itself
and waits for me to jump again.

Gutter Song

The drainpipe has a single tune
it only plays in heavy rain:
a hollow drum, a rattling spoon,
a gargle, then the drum again.
My mother says it needs repair.
I lie awake and hope it won't.
Some music only plays when things
are broken in the way they are.

Wet Dog

He went out brown and came back black,
the river's whole map on his back.
He shakes the weather on the floor,
the curtains and the kitchen door,
then sits, enormously pleased,
beside the stove, as if to say:
I fetched the rain. I brought it in.
You're welcome. It is yours today.

Forecast

The man on television points
at swirling shapes above the sea
and tells us, with a careful smile,
what tomorrow ought to be.
My grandmother just looks outside,
then at her aching knee,
and puts the washing back inside.
She's right more often. So is she.

Greenhouse

In my uncle's greenhouse
the rain is a roof of drums,
and the tomatoes listen,
red and round and serious,
as if the music were for them.
Water runs down every pane.
We sit on flowerpots, warm and dry,
inside the middle of the storm.

After the Rain

The street is shining like a plate
that someone has just washed.
The gardens drink. The snails come out
in their slow parade of shells.
Above the roofs a rainbow leans
against the hill and rests,
and every leaf holds one bright drop
as if it were a gift.

CHAPTER 2: City

Night Bus

The night bus hums its single note
through streets that have forgotten names.
A nurse is sleeping on her coat,
a boy is reading by the window frames.
We do not speak. We share the light,
the yellow seats, the patient hum,
the slow agreement of the night
that everybody will get home.

Lift

Eleven floors of strangers
standing close and facing forward,
watching the numbers change
as if they were a film.
Somebody's coffee. Somebody's perfume.
At seven, a woman says good morning
to all of us at once,
and the whole small box grows warmer.

Market

The fish stall sings in silver.
The apples argue red and green.
A man sells hats and tells the clouds
exactly what they mean.
The bread is warm, the honey slow,
the flowers bright as cheap balloons.
I spend my coins on all of it
and walk home rich by noon.

Pigeons

They own the square and know it,
strutting like small grey mayors
between the benches and the bins,
inspecting every crumb.
Nobody voted for them.
Nobody needed to.
They were here before the statues
and will stay when the statues go.

Crossing

Red man, green man, all of us
waiting on the kerb like runners,
a businessman, a child, a dog,
a grandmother with shopping bags.
The light changes. We step out together,
a small brave army crossing a river of cars,
and on the other side
we scatter back into our separate lives.

Rooftops

From the top of the car park
the city is a field of roofs:
chimneys, aerials, water tanks,
a garden someone planted on a garage,
a cat asleep on hot slate.
Down there it is all hurry.
Up here the pigeons and the washing
take the afternoon slowly.

Busker

Under the bridge a woman plays
the violin to passing feet.
Most people hurry. Some slow down.
A toddler stops, and sways, and stares,
until his father lifts him up
and carries him away still turning,
one small hand reaching for the sound
the way you reach toward a fire.

Laundrette

The washing machines turn their moons
in a row of round windows:
somebody's shirts, somebody's sheets,
a single yellow sock.
We sit on plastic chairs and wait,
reading old magazines,
everyone's week going round and round,
coming out clean.

Library

Inside, the city lowers its voice.
The traffic is a distant sea.
A man is reading about Spain,
a girl is learning chemistry,
and on the shelves ten thousand doors
stand closed but never locked,
waiting for anyone at all
to come in from the street and knock.

CHAPTER 3: Small Hours

Kettle

At three in the morning the kettle
is the only one awake with me.
It thinks about the problem
from the bottom up,
murmurs, rises, boils over with an answer,
then clicks off and offers it to me
in a white cup:
whatever happens, have some tea.

Window

The window holds the city
like a jar of fireflies:
one lamp, then another,
someone else who cannot sleep.
I raise my cup to all of them,
the strangers in their squares of light.
We do not know each other,
but we keep the same small watch tonight.

Clock

The kitchen clock is counting
in a language with one word.
It says it to the fridge, the chairs,
the bread bin and the dark.
It said it to my grandmother
when this was still her house,
and it will say it, patiently,
long after we are gone.

Fox

A fox crosses the empty road
as if she owned the night,
which, for these few hours, she does.
She stops beneath the streetlight,
looks straight up at my window,
decides I am not interesting,
and trots on toward the gardens
with her red flag held high.

Radio

Turned low, the radio is talking
to the lonely and the late:
a farmer listening in his barn,
a baker by her oven,
a lorry driver on the motorway.
The voice says the weather and the time
as softly as a lullaby
to people it will never meet.

Letter

I start a letter to an old friend
and get as far as "Dear."
Then everything I want to say
lines up behind the word
like passengers at a small station
waiting for a train.
I leave the page beside the cup.
Tomorrow I will write again.

Insomnia

Sleep is a cat that will not come
however softly I call.
It sits on the far side of the room
washing one paw, pretending
it has never heard its name.
The moment I stop calling,
it strolls across the blanket
and curls up warm against my back.

Milk Float

Before the sun, a humming cart
goes clinking down the street,
leaving white bottles on the steps
like notes that say: begin.
I never see the driver's face,
only the faint electric whine,
and the small glass promise on the step
that someone thought of us at five.

Morning

And then the birds begin,
one first, uncertain, then a crowd,
as if the dark had been a question
and the answer must be said out loud.
The paper boats are on the river.
The thunder has gone to bed.
I fold the night up like a letter
and send it on ahead.
