The Dragon Who Collected Maps
An original text written for the BookOrbit AI prototype.

CHAPTER 1: A Hoard of Paper

Most dragons collect gold. Pellinore collected maps.

He kept them in a vast cave beneath the Thornback Mountains, rolled and folded and stacked in towers that reached the ceiling. There were maps of kingdoms that no longer existed and maps of roads that had never been built. There were sea charts stained with salt, star maps painted on silk, a map of the underground rivers carved into a slab of slate, and a map of a single garden drawn by a child, which was his favourite. The child had drawn every flower, every stone and a very large cat, and written underneath: "This is everything."

Pellinore was old, green-grey and very large, and he was careful with fire. One sneeze in the wrong direction could destroy three hundred years of work. For this reason, he kept a bucket of sand beside every tower, and he never ate pepper.

Tamsin Reed came to the cave on a cold spring morning with a satchel, a pencil case and a great deal of courage. She was fourteen and an apprentice to the royal cartographer, and she had been sent to ask the dragon for a map. She had practised her speech for three days on the road. When she saw the dragon, she forgot all of it.

"Which map?" rumbled Pellinore, opening one golden eye.

"The map of the Unwritten Coast," said Tamsin, after a moment. "The Queen's ships are sailing there in the autumn. Every chart we have stops at the edge of the Pale Sea. After that, they just say 'Unknown.' Master Corwin says you have the only map that goes further."

The dragon was silent for so long that Tamsin wondered if he had fallen asleep. Dust drifted down from the towers of paper. Somewhere deep in the cave, water dripped.

"I had that map," he said finally. "It was drawn by a sailor who went further than anyone and came back. He gave it to me because he said I was the only one who would keep it safe. It was stolen forty years ago. It is the only map I have ever lost."

"Stolen? From a dragon?"

"From a dragon who was asleep," said Pellinore, with dignity. "Even dragons sleep. The thief left this."

He pushed a scrap of paper toward her with one claw. On it was a drawing of a feather, and three words in faded ink: "Maps should travel."

Tamsin studied it carefully, the way Master Corwin had taught her to study any document. The ink was brown, not black. The paper was thick and slightly rough, the kind that mapmakers used, not the kind used for letters.

"Whoever took it," she said slowly, "was a cartographer."

Pellinore opened his other eye.

CHAPTER 2: The Feather Sign

Tamsin did not go home. Instead, she sent a letter to Master Corwin and spent a week in the cave, helping Pellinore search his towers of maps for any clue about the thief.

She learned that the dragon could read fourteen languages and that he hated maps with sea monsters drawn on them. "Lazy," he said. "If you don't know what is there, write that you don't know." She learned how to unroll a three-hundred-year-old chart without cracking it, by warming it gently in the dragon's breath, and how to read the tiny marks that mapmakers hid in the corners of their work: initials, small animals, a flower, a star.

In the evenings, Pellinore told her stories about the maps. This one had guided an army that lost its way. That one had been drawn by a prisoner on the wall of his cell, and copied by the dragon before the wall was knocked down. Tamsin wrote everything in her notebook.

On the sixth day, she found the feather again.

It was drawn in the corner of a map of the river city of Lowmere, very small, beside the maker's name: Ilsa Quill.

"Quill," said Tamsin. "A feather."

Pellinore lifted his great head. "Ilsa Quill. She came here once, long ago, when she was young. She asked to copy my maps. She said that people were dying at sea because the good charts were locked away in castles and caves. I refused." He looked a little ashamed. "I did not like sharing in those days."

Tamsin and the dragon travelled to Lowmere together, which caused a great deal of shouting in the market square and one fainting merchant. Pellinore waited by the river while Tamsin asked questions. They found Ilsa Quill in a narrow house beside the water. She was very old now, with ink-stained fingers and bright, sharp eyes.

Her house was full of maps too, but not like the dragon's cave. These were pinned to every wall, spread across the table, folded into the bookshelves and even taped to the ceiling, and every one of them was covered in notes, crossings-out and additions in many different hands. They looked used, the way a well-loved coat looks used.

She looked out of her window at the dragon on the riverbank and did not seem surprised.

"I wondered when you would come," she said. "I have been waiting forty years to say sorry."

"Do you still have my map?" asked Pellinore, putting one enormous eye to the window.

"No," said Ilsa. "I gave it away."

CHAPTER 3: The Unwritten Coast

Ilsa Quill made tea, and Tamsin drank it on the riverbank while the old woman leaned out of the window and explained. She had taken the map because she believed knowledge should not be locked in a cave. She had copied it many times and given the copies to sailors, fishermen and explorers. She had asked only one thing in return: that each of them add what they discovered and send a copy back to her.

They had. Over forty years, the copies had come back marked with a new bay, a dangerous reef, a river mouth, a village where travellers were welcome, a spring of fresh water on a lonely island.

"The map did travel," she said. "And it grew."

She brought out a thick bundle of papers, bound with string. It was not one map, but dozens of pieces, drawn by many hands in many inks. Together they showed the whole of the Unwritten Coast, far beyond anything in Pellinore's original, with every safe harbour marked and every danger named.

The dragon studied it for a long time. Tamsin saw something change in his golden eyes.

"It is better than mine," he said quietly.

"It is yours as much as anyone's," said Ilsa. "You kept the first line safe until someone could use it."

Tamsin copied the coast carefully for the Queen's ships, working for three days at Ilsa's kitchen table. When she had finished, she added her own mark in the corner: a small dragon holding a feather.

Before she left Lowmere, Ilsa pressed something into her hand: a small brass pen with a nib shaped like a feather.

"For the next line," the old woman said. "There is always a next line."

The Queen's ships sailed that autumn and returned the next summer with every sailor safe. They brought their own corrections, which Tamsin added to the map with the feather pen.

When she returned to the Thornback Mountains to say goodbye, she found the entrance of the cave changed. Pellinore had carved a wide doorway and a sign above it: "The Library of Maps. Visitors welcome. Please do not sneeze."

"You are sharing them?" said Tamsin.

"Maps should travel," said the dragon. "But they should also come home sometimes, with new roads drawn on them."

And so they did. For the rest of his long life, sailors and travellers brought Pellinore their maps, and he added every new line to the great hoard beneath the mountains.
