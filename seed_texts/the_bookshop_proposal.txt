The Bookshop Proposal
An original text written for the BookOrbit AI prototype.

CHAPTER 1: The Crooked Shelf

Nora Bellamy's bookshop was called The Crooked Shelf because the shelves were, in fact, crooked. The building on Willow Street was two hundred years old, and its floors sloped gently toward the river, so that every book leaned a little to the left, as if trying to hear what the next book was saying.

Nora loved every crooked inch of it. She loved the bell above the door that rang slightly out of tune, and the smell of old paper and coffee, and the cat-shaped dent in the cushion of the reading chair, although there had not been a cat for years. She had worked there since she was seventeen, and when old Mr. Ambrose retired, he had sold her the business for one pound and a promise: "Keep the reading chair by the window. Somebody always needs it."

He was right. Students sat in it to read the books they could not afford. Old Mr. Patel sat in it every Wednesday to do the crossword. Once, a woman had sat in it for an entire afternoon, crying quietly over a novel, and had bought it on the way out without a word.

On a wet Monday in April, a man in a grey suit sat in the reading chair and did not read.

He looked at the ceiling. He tapped the walls. He measured the window with a small laser device that beeped.

"Can I help you find something?" Nora asked, in the voice she used for customers who were clearly not customers.

"Sam Ellery," he said, and held out a hand. "I'm an architect. My firm is designing the new riverside development. This building is on our plans."

"This building is a bookshop."

"For now," said Sam, and then had the grace to look embarrassed. "Sorry. That sounded worse than I meant. The owner of the building is selling. My client wants to turn the whole row into apartments. Glass balconies. A gym."

Nora felt the floor tilt a little more than usual.

"My lease ends in September," she said.

"I know," said Sam quietly. "I'm sorry. I didn't choose the site."

He stood, looked around at the leaning shelves, and paused at the poetry section. He took down a slim book, opened it, and smiled at something written inside the cover.

"Someone wrote a note in this," he said.

"People do," said Nora. "It's a second-hand shop. Books arrive with lives already in them. That one says 'To M, for the train to Edinburgh, love always, J.' I've always wondered if they got married."

"I hope so," said Sam, and he sounded as if he meant it.

He bought the book. She did not expect to see him again.

CHAPTER 2: Notes in the Margins

He came back on Thursday, and the Thursday after that.

Each time, he bought one book and left without discussing apartments. He bought a history of Venice, a book about trees, a detective novel with a broken spine. Nora told herself she did not care. She also noticed that she had started wearing her good green cardigan on Thursdays, and that Mr. Patel had noticed her noticing.

In the fourth week, Sam donated a box of the books back to the shop. "I've read them," he said. "Somebody else should." Inside the cover of the history of Venice, below an old handwritten note, was a new line in neat architect's capitals: "THE WINDOW SEAT GETS THE BEST LIGHT AT FOUR O'CLOCK. WHOEVER DESIGNED THIS PLACE KNEW WHAT THEY WERE DOING."

Nora found herself smiling at it for far too long.

She began to leave replies. She would slip a book onto the poetry shelf where he always looked first, with a note inside. In a book of garden essays, she wrote: "The floors slope because the river is patient. It has been pulling this street toward it for two hundred years." The following week, the book came back with a sketch of the building and an arrow: "Original oak beams. Could be saved. Nobody asked."

In a collection of letters, she wrote: "Mr. Ambrose says the building was a sail-maker's workshop first. That's why the ceilings are so high. They needed room to unroll the canvas." He replied with a drawing of the shop full of white sails, and the words: "Still a place for things that carry people somewhere."

They never mentioned the notes when he came in. They talked about the weather, about which novels were overrated, about Mr. Ambrose and the reading chair. But the notes continued, book after book, until the poetry shelf was full of their conversation, and Nora had to put a small sign on it saying "Not for sale."

In late July, the note said: "I have told my client that this building should stay. He is not happy. I am not sorry."

In early August, there was no note, and no Sam.

Thursday came and went. Then another. Nora wore the green cardigan both times and told herself it was because the weather was cool.

She read back through the notes on the poetry shelf one evening after closing, from the first to the last, like a short novel told in margins. She realised she did not know where he lived, or his phone number, or even which firm he worked for. All she had was his handwriting.

"He'll come back," said Mr. Patel the next Wednesday, without looking up from his crossword. "Nobody writes that much to a person and then just stops."

CHAPTER 3: The Last Page

On the first of September, Nora began packing the shop into boxes. She had found a small room above a launderette on the edge of town; it would hold perhaps a third of the books, and none of the crooked shelves. Customers came to say goodbye. Mrs. Haddad from the bakery brought cake. Mr. Patel brought his last crossword and left it unfinished on the reading chair, which Nora found more upsetting than anything else. Someone left flowers on the windowsill.

At four o'clock, when the light through the window turned gold, the door opened, and the bell rang its out-of-tune ring. Sam Ellery stood there, soaked by a sudden rain, holding a rolled set of plans.

"You stopped writing," said Nora, before she could stop herself.

"I was busy," he said. "I resigned from the riverside project. Then I spent five weeks convincing the city council that this row of buildings is historic. Which it is. The beams are from 1820, and Mr. Ambrose was right about the sail-makers. I found the records." He unrolled the plans on the counter, holding the corners down with books. "The council has listed the building. Nobody can knock it down now. And the owner has agreed to sell to a community trust instead, on one condition."

"What condition?"

"That the ground floor stays a bookshop."

Nora looked at the plans. The shelves were drawn exactly where they stood now, crooked angles and all, and someone had added a small note in architect's capitals beside the window: "READING CHAIR. DO NOT MOVE."

She found she was crying and laughing at the same time. Mrs. Haddad, who had not gone home, began to applaud. Mr. Patel picked up his unfinished crossword from the reading chair, sat down in it, and took out his pen.

"Well," he said, "I suppose I'd better finish this, then."

Nora turned back to Sam. "Why?" she asked. "You lost your job for a building with crooked floors."

"I lost a job," he said. "I've had several. I've only ever found one bookshop that leans toward the river."

"There's one more thing," said Sam. He took a book from his coat: the slim poetry collection he had bought on the very first day. "I never read the note inside the cover properly."

She opened it. Below the old faded handwriting about the train to Edinburgh, there was a new line.

"Nora Bellamy, would you have dinner with me? Please reply in the margins."

She took a pencil from the counter and wrote one word.
