CHAPTER 1: Not Plants, Not Animals

When most people think of fungi, they picture a mushroom on the forest floor. But the mushroom is only a small part of the organism, in the same way that an apple is only a small part of an apple tree. The mushroom is a fruiting body: a structure that the fungus produces to release spores, which spread and grow into new fungi. A single mushroom can release billions of spores, so small that they drift on the air like dust.

The main body of most fungi is hidden underground or inside the material they feed on. It is called the mycelium, and it is made of countless tiny threads called hyphae. Each hypha is much thinner than a human hair. Together, they form a branching network that can spread through soil, wood or leaves. If you lift a log in a damp forest, you may see the mycelium as a white, cotton-like web on the wood beneath. A single teaspoon of healthy forest soil can contain many metres of hyphae.

Hyphae grow at their tips, pushing forward through the soil and branching whenever they find food. When two hyphae from the same fungus meet, they can fuse together, so the network becomes more connected as it grows. In this way, a fungus can explore and feed on a large area without ever moving.

For a long time, fungi were classified as plants. They grow in one place, and many of them look a little like plants. But fungi cannot make their own food from sunlight, because they have no chlorophyll. Instead, they feed by releasing digestive chemicals into their surroundings and absorbing the nutrients that are released. In a sense, they digest their food outside their bodies. Their cell walls are made of chitin, the same tough material found in the shells of insects and crabs, rather than the cellulose found in plants.

Scientists now place fungi in their own kingdom. In fact, genetic studies show that fungi are more closely related to animals than to plants.

The kingdom of fungi is enormous. It includes mushrooms and moulds, the yeasts used to make bread and beer, and the organisms that cause athlete's foot. Some fungi are made of a single cell; others form networks that stretch for kilometres. Scientists have described more than one hundred thousand species, and they estimate that millions more have not yet been named. New species are found every year, in forests, in oceans, and even inside the bodies of other living things.

CHAPTER 2: The Great Recyclers

Imagine a forest where nothing ever rotted. Fallen leaves and dead trees would pile up year after year, and the nutrients locked inside them would never return to the soil. New plants would starve while the forest floor disappeared under dead wood. Fungi are one of the main reasons this does not happen.

Wood is difficult to break down. It contains cellulose and lignin, a tough substance that gives trees their strength and protects them from attack. Very few organisms can digest lignin, but certain fungi, known as white-rot fungi, produce special enzymes that can. As they feed, they release carbon, nitrogen and other nutrients back into the ecosystem, where plants can use them again. Together with bacteria and small animals, fungi turn dead material into rich soil. They are the great recyclers of the living world.

Many fungi also form partnerships with plants. In a mycorrhizal relationship, fungal hyphae grow around or into the roots of a plant. The fungus helps the plant absorb water and minerals, especially phosphorus, from a much larger area of soil than the roots could reach alone, because hyphae are far thinner and longer than roots. In return, the plant gives the fungus sugars made by photosynthesis. Most land plants form relationships like this, and many could not grow well without them. Some scientists believe that partnerships with fungi helped the first plants to move from water onto land hundreds of millions of years ago.

Not every fungus is a gentle partner. Some are parasites that feed on living plants, and the honey fungus described below can slowly kill the trees it grows on. Others live quietly inside plant leaves and stems without causing harm, and may even protect their hosts from insects or disease. The relationships between fungi and plants range from cooperation to conflict, and many lie somewhere in between.

Some researchers have found evidence that mycorrhizal networks can connect several trees, allowing carbon and chemical signals to pass between them. This idea is sometimes called the "wood wide web." Scientists still debate how important these connections are, and how much trees really share, but they agree that fungi play a central role in the health of forests.

Fungi can also grow to astonishing sizes. In the Blue Mountains of Oregon, a single individual honey fungus spreads underground across nearly ten square kilometres of forest. Most of it is invisible, but in autumn it produces clusters of golden mushrooms at the base of the trees it feeds on. Its age is estimated in thousands of years, making it one of the largest living organisms on Earth.

CHAPTER 3: Fungi and People

People have used fungi for thousands of years, often without knowing it. Bread rises because yeast, a single-celled fungus, feeds on sugars in the dough and releases bubbles of carbon dioxide, which make the dough light and soft. The same process, called fermentation, produces the alcohol in beer and wine. Fungi also give flavour and blue veins to certain cheeses, and they are used to make soy sauce and many other traditional foods.

One of the most important discoveries in medicine came from a fungus. In 1928, the Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming returned from a holiday to find that a mould had contaminated one of his bacterial cultures. Around the mould, the bacteria had died. Many scientists would have thrown the dish away. Fleming studied it. The mould was a species of Penicillium, and the substance it produced became penicillin, the first widely used antibiotic. It took more than ten years and the work of other scientists to produce penicillin in large quantities, but since then, antibiotics have saved many millions of lives.

Not all fungi are helpful. Some cause diseases in crops, such as the rusts that attack wheat, and others destroy stored food. Some cause infections in humans and animals. Many wild mushrooms are poisonous, and some deadly species look very similar to edible ones. For this reason, no one should eat a wild mushroom unless an expert has identified it.

Fungi have also caused some of history's great disasters. In the 1840s, a fungus-like organism destroyed the potato crops of Ireland, contributing to a famine in which around a million people died. Today, fungal diseases threaten bananas, coffee and wheat in many parts of the world, and plant scientists work constantly to breed resistant crops and track new outbreaks before they spread. Studying fungi is not only a matter of curiosity; it is a matter of food security.

Today, scientists are exploring new uses for fungi. Mycelium can be grown into shapes and dried to make light, natural packaging materials, leather-like fabrics and even building blocks. Unlike plastic foam, these materials can rot away harmlessly when they are no longer needed. Some fungi can break down plastics or help clean polluted soil, a process called mycoremediation. Researchers are also studying fungi for new medicines, new foods and new ways to protect crops.

The next time you see a mushroom after the rain, remember that it is only the visible tip of a hidden world: a vast network of threads beneath your feet, quietly recycling the forest and connecting the life around it.
