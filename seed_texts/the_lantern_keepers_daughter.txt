The Lantern Keeper's Daughter

CHAPTER 1: The Light on Gull Point

Every evening at a quarter to six, Wren Calloway climbed the one hundred and twelve steps of the Gull Point lighthouse with her father. Tobias Calloway carried the oil can and the soft cloth; Wren carried the matches and the logbook, because she was eleven and liked to write things down.

"Wind from the north-east," she wrote that October evening. "Three fishing boats out. Mrs. Pell's cat on the harbour wall again."

Her father laughed when he read it. "The cat is not an official observation."

"She is officially on the wall," Wren said.

Gull Point was a small town of grey houses, one bakery, one school and a harbour that smelled of salt and diesel. Everyone knew the lighthouse keeper and his daughter. When storms came, people looked up at the tower before they looked at the sky, because if the Calloway lamp was turning, the town felt safe.

The lamp room at the top of the tower was Wren's favourite place in the world. The great glass lens stood in the middle of it like a giant beehive made of crystal, taller than her father. When the lamp was lit, the lens gathered the small flame and threw it twenty miles out to sea. Her father had taught her how to polish each prism without leaving a fingerprint, and how to wind the clockwork that turned the lens once every eight seconds.

Wren's mother had died when Wren was four. She remembered very little of her: a blue scarf, a song about a boat with paper sails, the smell of oranges. Her father did not talk about her often, but on the top shelf of the lamp room he kept a small brass compass that had been hers. Wren was allowed to hold it on her birthday and on no other day.

That evening, as the great lens began to turn, a car drove up the cliff road. A man in a city coat stepped out with a leather folder under his arm. He looked at the tower the way a person looks at a broken clock.

"Mr. Calloway?" he called. "I'm from the Coastal Authority. We need to talk about the future of this light."

Wren watched her father's hands go still on the brass rail. Below them the sea moved slowly, as if it were listening too.

"The future," her father said, "is that it keeps shining."

The man smiled without warmth. "The future is automatic, I'm afraid. A machine can do this job now. In the spring, the keeper's post will close, and you will be offered a position in the city office."

"I don't want an office," said her father. "I have a lighthouse."

When the car had gone, they finished the evening checks in silence. Wren opened the logbook again. Under the note about the cat she wrote, in careful letters: "Man from the city. Wants to take the light away. We will not let him."

CHAPTER 2: Letters in the Lamp Room

The news spread through Gull Point faster than a winter fog. By Saturday, everyone at the bakery was talking about the automatic light.

"A machine doesn't wave at the boats," said Mrs. Pell, who owned the famous cat.

"A machine doesn't climb the stairs with soup when someone is ill," said Mr. Okafor from the harbour office.

"A machine doesn't notice when a boat is late," said old Captain Harlow, who had fished these waters for fifty years. Everyone went quiet after that, because everyone knew that was the most important thing of all.

Wren listened from the corner table with her best friend, Mabel Finch. Mabel was practical. She had a notebook too, but hers was full of lists.

"We need evidence," Mabel said. "Grown-ups like evidence. If the light is important, we have to prove it."

So the two girls began an investigation. Every day after school they walked along the harbour with their notebooks. They asked every fisherman how many times the Gull Point light had guided them home. They counted the ships in the old logbooks, which went back sixty years. They copied out every storm, every rescue and every night when the keeper had stayed awake until the last boat was in. At the town library, they found an article from 1974 about a ferry that had nearly hit the rocks in a storm, saved because the keeper saw its lights and fired a warning flare.

The keeper in 1974 had been Wren's grandfather.

In the lamp room, looking for older logbooks, Wren found something else: a tin box behind the oil cans. Inside were letters, tied with a blue ribbon. They were addressed to her father, and they were written in her mother's handwriting.

Wren knew she should not read them. She read only the first line of the top letter: "Toby, if the light ever goes out, remember that you are the reason it was lit."

She put the letters back very carefully. Her heart was beating fast. That night she could not sleep. She lay listening to the beam pass over her window, once every eight seconds, as regular as breathing.

At breakfast, she said, "Dad, did Mum like the lighthouse?"

Her father put down his cup. For a long moment he said nothing. Then he said, "Your mother said it was the only building in the world that existed just to help strangers. She used to sit on the gallery and count the boats coming home. She said every light in a window was somebody waiting."

"Then we have to save it," said Wren.

"Wren, the Authority has decided."

"Decisions can change," she said. "Mabel says we need evidence. We have sixty years of it."

Her father looked at her for a long time. Then, for the first time in weeks, he smiled.

"Then you had better bring it to the town meeting," he said.

CHAPTER 3: The Night of the Long Storm

The meeting at the town hall was set for the first Tuesday in March. Wren and Mabel had prepared a folder: fifty-three statements from fishermen, the 1974 newspaper article, and a hand-drawn map showing every rock between Gull Point and the northern channel. Mabel had typed a list of contents. Wren had decorated the cover with a drawing of the lighthouse and, after some argument, a small cat.

But on the Monday night, the storm came.

It came from the north-east, exactly as Wren had written in her logbook so many times. The wind screamed around the tower. Rain struck the glass like handfuls of gravel. At ten o'clock, the power failed across the whole town, and the new electric test lamp that the Authority had installed on the gallery went dark.

The old lens did not. Tobias Calloway had kept the oil lamp ready, the way his father had taught him. Wren held the torch while he lit it, and together they wound the clockwork that turned the great lens. The beam swept out across the black water.

Down in the town, people stood at their windows in the dark. One by one, they saw the light turning on the cliff, and one by one they lit candles of their own.

At half past eleven, Wren saw a light below the cliffs: a small boat, far too close to the rocks. Her father fired the warning flare. The red star burst over the water. The boat turned, slowly, slowly, and slipped into the safe channel toward the harbour.

The boat belonged to the man from the Coastal Authority. He had been driving back from the city when the roads flooded, and a fisherman had offered to bring him round by sea.

He came to the lighthouse the next morning, still wet, holding his leather folder. He did not smile his cold smile. He climbed all one hundred and twelve steps and stood in the lamp room for a long time, looking at the old lens and the brass compass on the shelf.

"Machines need electricity," he said finally. "People don't."

At the town hall meeting, Wren read out the fishermen's statements in a voice that shook only a little. The man from the Authority spoke last. He told the room what had happened in the storm. The decision was changed. The Gull Point light would keep its keeper, and a training post would be created for a young assistant.

"When you're older," said her father on the walk home, "the post might suit someone who writes things down."

That evening, Wren climbed the stairs with her father as usual. On the top shelf, beside the brass compass, she placed the folder of evidence. Then she opened the logbook and wrote: "Wind calm. Light shining. Mrs. Pell's cat on the harbour wall. Officially."
